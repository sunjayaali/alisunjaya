## The Dunning-Kruger Effect

In 1995, a man named McArthur Wheeler walked into two banks in Pittsburgh and robbed them. The strange this wasn't that he robbed two banks, it was he apparently believed he had found a way to become invisible to security cameras. His method was lemon juice. Wheeler had learned that lemon juice can be used as an invisible ink. If you write something with lemon juice and heat the paper, the writing becomes visible. Somehow, he took that idea and concluded that putting lemon juice on his face would make him invisible to cameras.

Later, when police showed him surveillance footage, Wheeler was reportedly confused. He couldn't understand why his face was clearly visible. The story caught the attention of Cornell psychologists **David Dunning** and **Justin Kruger**. They became interested in a simple question:

> How can someone be so incompetent at something that they don't realize they're incompetent?

That question led to their famous 1999 paper, "*Unskilled and Unaware of It*".

## When Confidence Comes Before Understanding

I've talked with several vibe coders who were really proud of the apps they had built. Until I saw the code behind them, the UI/UX, and the functionality. It was all just a mess. They fix one thing and break something else. They add a feature without understanding the existing code. And most importantly, they don't actually solve the real problem. Yet they were still extremely proud of what they had built.

What caught my attention wasn't that they used AI. It was the confidence they had in their work despite not having the knowledge to recognize its problems. And that's where the Dunning-Kruger effect comes in. When you're new to something, you can become confident long before you have enough knowledge to properly evaluate your own work.

## I've Been There Too

And honestly, I've experienced this myself. There were times when I learned a new technology, built something with it, and felt really proud of what I had made. I knew just enough to make it work, so I thought I understood the technology. But looking back, I realize I didn't. I was proud because I had successfully built something with a technology I had just learned, not because I actually understood it deeply.

I think that's a normal part of learning. The important part is being able to look back and realize that your confidence was ahead of your actual understanding. Because once you recognize that, you can start learning differently.

## The Learning Curve

This is where the Dunning-Kruger effect becomes interesting when you apply it to learning something new. You learn a little, build something, and suddenly feel like you understand the technology. Your confidence goes up quickly because you haven't encountered enough problems yet to realize how much you don't know. This is where I think a lot of vibe coders get stuck. They learn how to prompt AI, get an application running, and immediately feel like they've mastered the technology. But then comes the uncomfortable part of learning, it's understanding the problems they're solving.

As you gain more experience, you start noticing things you couldn't see before. You realize your architecture is bad. Your abstractions don't make sense. Your code is difficult to maintain. Fixing one thing breaks another. And sometimes, after all that work, you realize you weren't even solving the right problem.

The goal isn't to avoid that drop in confidence. You actually want to reach it. It's the point where you become aware of the gaps in your knowledge. I'd rather be someone who realizes how much they still have to learn than someone who mistakes a small amount of success and claims mastery.
